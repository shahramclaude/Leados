# ADR-0240 — Major-incident command & notification-clock register: declare on a written criterion, hand over on the record, and never release command by resolving the incident

- **Status:** accepted and built — Schema v194, route `#/incidents`, `RULE-MIC-01` on the shared spine (153), CSV scope #181, demo seed, insights bucket (MIC-01..03), focused suite 53/53, head pins across seven neighbouring suites moved to v194, standalone rebuilt.
- **Date:** 2026-11-17
- **Deciders:** An incident is *declared* on a written criterion, never inferred from the loudness of a channel. Command is *held by one named hand*, and it changes only by a recorded assumption or a recorded handover. Every notification obligation carries a declared clock with a written source, or it carries none and says so. Resolution and release are two acts, and the second cannot be inferred from the first.
- **Schema:** **v194** (v193 → v194). Three additive collections, all **empty at upgrade**: `micIncidents`, `micActs`, `micMarkers`. No incident, clock, commander, notification or deadline is inferred from anything an upgraded workspace already holds — an operational case on file is not an incident somebody declared. Five bounded conventions: `micAutoPropagate` (pause switch), `micClosingWindowMinutes` 120 [5–1440], `micChaseDays` 1 [0–14], `micLapsedAskDays` 1 [0–14], `micAcknowledgeVarianceMinutes` 0 [0–120].
- **Surface:** `#/incidents` — Live (command state, intervals, obligations, accountable seat), Clocks (every declared clock, its source, its exact deadline and remaining minutes), Lines (each response duty and whether anyone actually accepted it), History (the immutable act ledger in order), Method (the generated figure `app/assets/incident-command-method.jpg`). Seven dialogs: declare, assume command, hand over, record notification, close incident, stand down, change severity.
- **Code:** `incidentCommandRegistry.js` (closed vocabularies, MIC_RIGHTS, limits, section titles, method copy), `incidentCommandSchema.js` (fail-closed loaders, exact instant and whole-minute parsing), `incidentCommand.js` (pure reader + insights signals MIC-01..03), `incidentCommandActions.js` (seat-checked acts, all writes through `store.update`), `incidentCommandRules.js` (`RULE-MIC-01`), `incidentCommandReport.js` (CSV #181 rows), `views/incidents.js` (panel). Wired through `schema.js`, `actions.js`, `main.js` (nav, badge, renderer, ten route aliases, help text), `accessGateway.js`, `seatRegistry.js`, `propagationContracts.js`/`postCommit.js`, `pausePermitRegistry.js`, `csv.js` (#181, group `operations`), `insights.js`, `demo.js`.
- **Tests:** [`incident-command.test.mjs`](../../tests/app/incident-command.test.mjs) — **53/53 pass**.
- **Builds on:** ADR-0140/0141 (the ONE matrix engine — `matrixJointApex` decides the accountable seat and is never re-implemented), ADR-0118/0159 (the append-only act ledger and refusal-with-reason), ADR-0209 (work & rest — related but distinct: that ledger records *shifts worked*; this register records *command held during a live incident*), and the standing rules that a figure is derived on read, that a refusal prints its reason, and that a passed deadline is never converted into a met one.

## Context

Every vendor in this category — incident.io, PagerDuty, FireHydrant — sells the same shape: declare from chat, one incident commander, a role model, a timeline, an auto-drafted postmortem. Their published guides state the bar plainly, and the Major Incident Management Process Guide is the clearest statement of it: declare early on **written** criteria, name **one** commander who does **not** fix the problem, ladder severity with "when unsure take the higher", escalate on security involvement, and ask afterwards for *time-to-declaration* and *time-to-commander*. The audit-trail comparison material is equally clear about the evidence shape a SOC2 CC7.3/CC7.4 or ISO 27001 A.12.4.1 reader expects: timestamp, actor, action, state transition, decision record, metadata — on an **immutable** timeline written at the time, not retro-edited.

What none of them does, and what a leadership system must do, is three things:

1. **Record the obligation to tell someone as a declared clock with a named owner**, rather than as a message somebody sent in a channel. GDPR Art. 33 and NIS2 Art. 23 are *duties to notify within a period*; a product that treats "we posted in #incidents" as satisfying that duty is guessing. No jurisdiction table is shipped here, because inventing one would be worse than refusing: **the clock is declared from the instrument, or it does not exist.**
2. **Carry command hand-over with accountability on the reporting graph.** Who was in command at 03:40, and who is above every response line right now. In a matrix that seat is not one person's manager and cannot be picked.
3. **Refuse to close the record without its review.** Resolving the technical fault is not releasing the command structure. A bridge left nominally open for four days is a real, common and completely invisible failure.

Verified before building: no `mic*` vocabulary existed in `app/` or `tests/`; `#/incidents` was unregistered; `RULE-MIC-01` did not exist (`RULE-INC-01` and `RULE-MIA-01` were both taken). The nearest neighbours are `cases` (a general record, whose `detectedAt`/`declaredAt` are generic field names and not incident machinery) and the CSV scope name `incidents`, previously unused.

## Decision

**A declaration is a written criterion applied at a recorded instant; command is one hand at a time; a deadline is computed from the origin the declaration named; resolution and release are separate and ordered.**

1. **The declaration is the record.** An incident carries a title, a severity drawn from the numbered ladder, and a **non-empty written criterion** naming why that rung applies. A declaration instant before the detection instant is refused; a severity with no readable criterion is refused. Severity can be raised by an act at any time and is **never silently lowered** — the register prints the peak it reached and the act that raised it.

2. **Command moves by act, and only by act.** `assume` makes the actor the holder. `transfer` names a **successor who becomes the holder — never the actor**, because reading a handover as the outgoing commander's assumption reverses the one thing the screen exists to show. A transfer from nobody is refused; a transfer to the current holder is refused; a second assumption by a different hand is refused, because command moves by transfer and not by grabbing. **The commander cannot also lead a response line** — the commander who fixes is the single most common real-world failure, and it is a hard refusal here.

3. **The clock is declared, or it does not exist.** Each `(party, basis)` obligation may name one of the incident's own declared clocks. A clock carries a **source** from a closed vocabulary (`statute`, `contract`, `regulatory-guidance`, `internal-standard`), a **whole-minute period** in `[1, 10080]`, an **origin** (`awareness` or `declaration`), and a written basis. The exact deadline is `originAt + period`, where the origin is the instant the declaration **named** — not the convenient one. An obligation with no clock reads **`no-clock`** and is given **no deadline**; it is never silently treated as 72 hours. A source outside the vocabulary, a fractional period or a missing basis is refused at the door.

4. **Both facts survive a late notification.** Recording a notification stores the instant, the reference it went out under, and the actor. If it went out after the deadline the obligation reads **`recorded-late`** and keeps **both** the instant and the exact minutes it overran. A notification with no reference, one dated in the future, or one dated before the declaration is refused. A second record on the same obligation is refused — the first record stands. Time alone never converts a passed deadline: `lapsed` is printed with its duration and stays `lapsed`.

5. **Resolution is not release.** `resolve` records that the operational consequence has stopped, with written evidence. `stand-down` records that command was released, with a reason. A resolved incident with no stand-down act reads **`command-standing` forever** — at any later instant, with nobody acting, the register still says the bridge is open. A stand-down before a close is refused. A stood-down incident takes no further acts.

6. **The accountable seat is computed, never chosen.** It is `matrixJointApex` over every response line lead **and** the commander, through the one matrix engine. A genuine diamond prints **every** candidate and names none as accountable. Where the only seat above the lines is the workspace itself, the register prints **`leader-held`** rather than dressing the operator up as a line manager. `no-authority` is a printable finding about the graph, not a gap to be filled.

7. **`RULE-MIC-01` asks, once, pausably, and only the hand that can act.** Four conditions: a lapsed notification deadline, a notification inside its closing window, a live incident with no commander, and a closed incident whose command was never released. The stand-down ask goes to the **current commander** — the only hand `standDown` will accept — and falls back to the joint apex above the lines only where the commander is unreachable, and then **says so in a printed observation**. Signatures are opaque (`mic1:lapsed:<incidentId>:<notifId>`, `mic1:closing:<incidentId>:<notifId>:<day>`, `mic1:no-commander:<incidentId>`, `mic1:command-standing:<incidentId>`); no incident title, party or minute count appears in a delegated task, because a delegation is readable above the seat it is sent to. Capped at four asks per commit. **An ask whose condition persists but whose addressee moved is released and carried over**, never closed-and-refiled in the same pass: that churn would show a leader a phantom "done" for a condition that never cleared. Observing an unreachable graph writes an identity-free marker and asks nobody.

8. **The CSV is aggregate and identity-free.** Scope #181 prints counts only — incidents, states, severities, notification states by party, lapsed and late totals, the detection-to-declaration median above its floor — with **blank-with-reason** where a figure cannot be computed, never zero-as-reassurance. No incident title, no id, no person, no instrument text, no reference.

## Defects found while writing the suite, and fixed

A suite that only confirms the design has not tested it. Six real product defects were found by writing these 53 assertions, and each is now pinned:

- **`components.table()` reads `col.render`, not `col.cell`.** Thirty-eight columns on the new panel were silently ignored and the page printed raw reader fields. The `[object Object]` placeholder was **rendered on screen** and survived the first render check; it was caught only by reading the row's HTML back. Fixed by converting all 38 columns to `render`.
- **A phantom ask cycle.** The reconciler treated "the addressee of this condition changed" as "the condition cleared": it closed the open task and re-filed the same signature in the same pass. After a commander assumed command, the lapsed ask read `done` with a fresh `active` duplicate beside it. Fixed by **carrying an ask across a move** (rewriting `personId` and the link's source) and closing only on genuine clearance.
- **The operator seat could not hold command consistently.** `assign` and `transfer` validated against `people` only, so the workspace's own operator seat could `assume` command and then be told *"That person is not on the active roster"* on the next act — a door that lies about why it refused. Fixed with one shared `isLegalHand` that knows the operator seat is a legitimate commander but **not** a roster person.
- **The apex printed the raw id `leader`** in the command column, in front of a chief executive. Fixed to the codebase convention (`settings.leaderName || 'Workspace leader'`) already used by `concordance`, `jurisdiction` and `envoy`.
- **A block-level `<div>` nested inside a `<span>`** in the accountable-seat cell — invalid HTML that the browser reflows, so the rendered result drifts from the markup. Fixed.
- **The declare dialog patched state after the fact.** The dialog declared a clock and an obligation in one act, could not know the generated key, and so wrote a literal `'__first__'` then reached into `ctx.api.store.update` to back-patch it — a stub-shaped workaround inside a view. Fixed at the door: `incidentDeclare` accepts the positional token `first` and resolves it to the first declared clock. Any *other* unknown key is still refused, never silently dropped.

## Alternatives considered

- **Option A — infer the deadline from an entity-type table (all breaches: 72 hours).** Rejected outright. A wrong default is worse than a printed absence: it would let a leader believe a duty was tracked when the period was invented. The register has no jurisdiction table, and `no-clock` is a first-class state.
- **Option B — treat the stand-down as part of resolve.** Rejected: it is precisely the collapse that makes a bridge left open for four days invisible. Two acts, ordered, printed separately.
- **Option C — let the commander also lead a line** ("player-coach", the common small-team compromise). Rejected as a door refusal. The commander who fixes loses the room; the register makes that a wall, not advice.
- **Option D — auto-resolve the incident when a linked operational case closes.** Rejected: an incident's operational end is a commander's declaration with evidence, not a side effect of another record's state. Nothing here reads another collection.

## Consequences

- **Easier:** *time-to-declaration* and *time-to-commander* are now exact stored facts rather than postmortem archaeology; the lapsed-obligation question is answerable from the record without trusting a chat export; a handover has an actor and an instant; and "did we ever actually release command?" is a printed state, not a memory.
- **Harder / debt:** clocks and criteria are *declarations*, so a lazy declaration produces a lazy record — the register can refuse an unsourced clock but cannot force an honest one. The closing-window chase is bounded per commit (four asks), so a record with many incidents will ask over several commits. `command-standing` is deliberately loud and stays loud; a team that never uses stand-down will see it every day, which is the intended pressure but is real noise.
- **Revisit when:** a workspace needs a *pre-declared* clock library it can reuse across declarations (a template that still carries a written source and never an inferred default); or when postmortem obligations (the 3-day SEV-1 / 5-day SEV-2 bar) justify their own ledger. Concrete trigger: a leader asks to be chased on the *review* rather than the release.

## Benchmark

Bar taken from the fetched Major Incident Management Process Guide, incident.io's audit-trail comparison, and the PagerDuty / FireHydrant category pages, together with the SOC2 CC7.3/CC7.4 and ISO 27001 A.12.4.1 evidence field list those pages carry.

**Cleared natively:** written declaration criteria with a numbered ladder; one named commander who does not fix; command handed over on record with actor and instant; notification obligations as declared clocks never auto-satisfied; an immutable timestamped act ledger; no score on any person; time-to-declaration and time-to-commander printed exactly.

**Exceeds the category:** the deadline must name the origin it measures from; a missing clock is a printed state rather than an assumed 72 hours; a late notification keeps both facts; resolution and release are separate and the second is refused before the first; the stand-down ask is addressed to the hand that can actually act.

**Not claimed:** no hands-on vendor run and no environment comparison, so product parity is **[BENCHMARK-UNVERIFIED]**. Nothing here integrates with a paging provider; declaration is manual by design.
